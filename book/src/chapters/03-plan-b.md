# Plan B: how close we can get to balance without raising taxes

## The guardrails

Pay restraint is negotiated, never legislated. Health savings come from payment, drug-coverage and home-care rules, not from cutting services or charging patients more. Nothing is downloaded to municipalities, and no tax or fee goes up. Plan B counts only on top of Plan A: the government's own targets, $590 million rising to $882 million by 2029-30, are still delivered first.

Each lever below has a card with four ratings. The saving meter is filled in proportion to the modelled 2029-30 saving, full at $200 million a year. The other three meters read from low to high; they are my judgments, with the reasons on the card.

## The five levers

::: {.card code="B1"}
::: {.rail label="What"}
Multi-year settlements one point a year below whatever the plan assumes, for increases that are still unsettled when Budget 2027-28 is tabled. Only open increases count. The civil service's 2% on 1 April 2027 is set by an agreement running to 31 March 2028 [33], so the civil service counts only from April 2028. The Nova Scotia Health and IWK agreements expired on 31 October 2025 [34], and the teachers' agreement on 31 August 2026 [35]. I have not verified where that bargaining stands now. Finance has not published its wage assumption (question 2 in chapter 5), so I cannot say what rate this means. The payroll bases are approximate: they are not reduced for the government's own workforce cuts, which could make this lever up to about $11 million smaller in 2029-30.
:::

::: {.rail label="Guardrails"}
Negotiated, never legislated. The Bill 148 remedy is settled and booked first [22]. British Columbia's 2012 mandate funded raises only from real savings that did not cut service or shift costs to the public [37]; any savings used that way would be separate from Plan A's health measures, the other levers and the Fiscal Stability Plan. Its 2014 five-year mandate added a dividend when growth beat forecast and reached agreements covering 99.9% of unionized employees [38].
:::
:::

::: {.card code="B2"}
::: {.rail label="What"}
Physician services grow 1.5 points a year slower than the plan assumes, from April 2027, net to the Province. The physician agreement ends 31 March 2027, and its terms continue until a new one is signed [36]. The new one could carry a modest rate path, a published physician-services budget, and a joint process with Doctors Nova Scotia on low-value fee codes. Alberta's 2022 agreement gave doctors half of any savings from keeping cost growth below population and complexity growth [39]; if half were shared back that way, 2029-30 would be about $598 million instead of $560 million.
:::

::: {.rail label="Guardrails"}
No unilateral fee cuts, no hard cap and no special law. "Low-value services" means fee codes agreed with Doctors Nova Scotia, not services taken out of insured coverage.
:::
:::

::: {.card code="B3"}
::: {.rail label="What"}
Drug programs grow one point a year slower, through formulary listing and generic management under national price negotiation. The base is about $483 million on the September forecast basis [10][1].
:::

::: {.rail label="Guardrails"}
No change to Seniors' or Family Pharmacare premiums, co-pays or eligibility, and no currently covered patient left without coverage or paying a difference. In 2016 a planned seniors' pharmacare premium increase was halted as "too much, too soon" [40].
:::
:::

::: {.card code="B4"}
::: {.rail label="What"}
Home care grows one point a year slower, through one province-wide assessment standard and by verifying the hours providers report. In November 2017 the Auditor General found that reported service hours were not properly checked [41]. In its last regular follow-up of the 2017 audit, as of October 15, 2021, Nova Scotia Health had completed the recommendation to verify provider reports, including hours; the Department of Seniors and Long-term Care had not [58]. I have not verified the current implementation status or a measured error rate; Finance should publish both. Until then this lever is a hypothesis. The base is about $503 million on the September forecast basis, assuming the forecast's home-support pressure sits in this line [10][1].
:::

::: {.rail label="Guardrails"}
No one loses assessed hours, eligibility does not tighten, and Plan A's Home First money for home care is untouched.
:::
:::

::: {.card code="B5"}
::: {.rail label="What"}
In the 13 departments the consultation tool does not label protected, other than Municipal Affairs, lines are cut by up to 5%. Held whole: housing and public housing, signed Jobs Fund contracts, arts and culture, independent officers and prosecutors, Access Nova Scotia counters, the line that pays the seniors' property tax and heating rebates, student aid, university and community college grants, apprenticeship and skills training, inspection and enforcement, wildfire protection and the cyber security unit [32][10]. That leaves about $980 million of department operations and smaller grants. Beyond Plan A's entries in the tool, 5% of it is about $23 million on this year's base.
:::

::: {.rail label="Guardrails"}
No downloading to municipalities, no line cut by more than 5% in all, nothing mid-year or retroactive; Ontario reversed its retroactive 2019 cuts to municipal funding [42]. Operations are cut by attrition, head office first. The government's own reductions and Plan A's other measures also fall on these departments, and I cannot reconcile them line by line until the ledger is published. So B5 is an upper bound: it counts only the room the ledger shows is left under 5%. With none left, 2029-30 is about $587 million.
:::
:::

## What Plan B adds up to

| $ millions | 2027-28 | 2028-29 | 2029-30 |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| Plan A deficit | 1,150 | 1,050 | 856 |
| B1 Pay: open settlements one point a year below plan | −23 | −85 | −150 |
| B2–B4 Physicians, drugs, home care | −33 | −68 | −104 |
| B5 Departments to 5%, learning and trades held whole | −14 | −24 | −25 |
| Interest saved (illustrative 4.2%, half in the first year) | −1 | −7 | −17 |
| **Plan B deficit** | **1,079** | **867** | **560** |
| Plan B without B5 | 1,094 | 893 | 587 |

Figures are rounded, so columns may not add exactly. Plan B leaves 65% of the plan's 2029-30 deficit, and net debt still reaches about 45% of the economy.

::: careful
**This does not reach zero.** Plan B takes 2029-30 to about $560 million, or about $587 million if no department room is left. It would be roughly $470 million if increases already open from 2025 and 2026 also settled lower, and about $571 million if the wage bases shrink with the workforce cuts. Reaching zero with these levers would need settlements about 4.5 points a year below the plan for three years. If the plan assumes about 2% a year, the last rate in both the civil service and physician agreements [33][36], that means pay cuts. The alternative, about $560 million more of cuts in 2029-30, would reach the front-line services I protect. I propose neither. If this year's overruns recur, 2027-28 is about $1.29 billion even with Plan B, and the Bill 148 remedy sits outside every figure here [22].
:::

| B2 Physician services | 1,493 | 1.5 points a year slower | 73 |

Bases are 2025-26 audited payroll (health authorities [18][34]), a derived teachers' salary base [62], the 2026-27 civil-service salary estimates, and 2026-27 estimates plus the September forecast's changes for the health lines [10][1]. They grow with the plan's own departmental spending before Fiscal Stability Plan savings, 7.8% by 2029-30 [2]. The plan publishes neither its wage assumption nor its health growth paths, so each lever is measured against a baseline only Finance can confirm. These are my costing assumptions, not published figures.

## Who it asks the most of

Health workers, teachers and, from 2028, civil servants, through smaller raises. Physicians, through slower growth in what the Province pays. Department staff, through attrition, and the recipients of smaller grants outside the protected lines. B5 makes no further cut to university or NSCC grants, apprenticeship or skills training; Plan A's administrative savings there keep their teaching and service conditions. If its guardrails hold, it asks nothing more of patients, students, municipalities or taxpayers.

## Timing

Decisions are front-loaded into 2027-28 where possible, through multi-year agreements and department budgets. The civil-service increases open again in April 2028, the last round ended in arbitration, and the health levers need delivery every year. The fixed election date has been repealed, and the Charter limits the legislature elected in 2024 to five years from the date fixed for the return of its writs [43].

## Why Plan A remains my main proposal

Plan B depends on bargaining the government does not control. Imposed restraint has cost more later: Ontario's Financial Accountability Office projected $9.7 billion of savings from its 2019 wage law (2019 to 2026-27), then estimated $13.7 billion of wage adjustments (2022-23 to 2027-28) after a court struck it down [44][45]. Research across OECD countries finds that governments which cut deficits are not systematically voted out, but that spending cuts lower government approval [46][47]. The first task is still Plan A's: deliver the $590 million openly and stop the overruns. Plan B shows how far restraint could go beyond that, and where it stops.
