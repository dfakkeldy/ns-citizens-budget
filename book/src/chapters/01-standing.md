# Where things stand

## The numbers

::: {.rail label="This year"}
The 2026-27 deficit is now forecast at $1.49 billion after contingency, $195.7 million worse than the budget passed in April [1]. Health (+$125.3 million) and Seniors and Long-term Care (+$62.0 million) drove most of the increase.
:::

::: {.rail label="Next year"}
The plan for 2027-28 is a deficit of $1.149 billion [2]. It already counts on $590.0 million of Fiscal Stability Plan savings ($622.3 million in the February budget, before the March reinstatements). No public document says, department by department, where they will come from.
:::

::: {.rail label="The $590 million"}
Table 3.1 of the Addendum gives one figure per year [2]. When the Addendum reinstated 2026-27 grants, it lowered every year's figure by the same $32.3 million. I read that as each year's figure being the total reduction counted that year, with this year's measures counted again. On that reading, up to $272.6 million of the $590 million is this year's reductions continuing, and at least $317.4 million is new. The table does not show its parts, so I ask Finance to confirm. If all of this year's reductions continue and the $85.5 million I propose in Plan A is delivered, about $231.9 million is still to be identified under these assumptions. My $85.5 million is a proposal, not a government figure, and I could find no public document that itemizes the rest. On the other reading, the figure would be about $504.5 million.
:::

::: {.rail label="Debt"}
Net debt reaches 42.8% of the economy in 2027-28, 45.0% in 2028-29 and 46.0% in 2029-30 [2], against the government's own planning guardrail of under 40% [3]. Even the government's own line without the long-term-care accounting change passes 40% from 2028-29 [2].
:::

::: {.rail label="Over budget"}
Cabinet approved $1.90 billion of additional appropriations in 2025-26 without a vote in the House; $490 million of that was capital-purchase authority [5]. The Restructuring Costs line came in at $1,144.1 million against a $551.5 million estimate [4].
:::

::: {.rail label="Credit"}
S&P rates Nova Scotia A+ with a negative outlook, after a January 2026 downgrade [6]. Morningstar DBRS wrote in February that "there is no plan to return to balance" [7]. At the illustrative 4.2% financing rate used in this submission, each $100 million of deficit avoided saves about $4.2 million of interest in each full year after (half that in the first year). Coupons on the Province's 2025-26 bond issues ranged from 3.85% to 4.75% [3].
:::

## Why the consultation tool can't balance it

The online tool locks taxes, fees and federal transfers, lets departments move only 5% either way, and lets Health go only up. Every cut the tool allows adds up to about $508 million, against the tool's deficit of $1,236 million [32]. Nobody can balance the budget inside the government's own exercise. So this submission has two tiers: a plan the government can adopt now (Plan A), and a priced path toward balance that raises no taxes (Plan B).

## Credit where it's due

Nova Scotia had record housing starts in 2025 (9,760), though starts were down 29.2% in January to August 2026 compared with the same months of 2025 [12][48]. Halifax's average approval time fell from 20.8 to 9.8 months for the residential applications sampled in the Canadian Home Builders' Association studies, a sign of improvement rather than proof of what caused it [13]. More than 15,500 households have been helped with a rent supplement since 2023, and master leasing cut the number of people living in emergency hotels by 86% [12][31]. And the government listened when grant cuts hit people with disabilities, seniors, and African Nova Scotian and Indigenous students: it reversed $53.6 million of them in March [2].

## How I count savings

The plan already counts on the $590 million, so I sort every measure into one of four kinds:

- **Toward the target**: it names where part of the $590 million can come from. It helps make the plan deliverable but does not lower the published deficit.
- **Stops overruns**: it keeps costs from running above the plan, as Health and Seniors' care did this year. It is measured against the risk, not the plan.
- **Below the plan**: it lowers the deficit beneath the government's own figure.
- **Rules**: disclosure and accountability. Worth nothing directly, but they keep the rest from slipping.

Every figure is my estimate, not a guarantee, and each is counted once. Plan A's main source figures and selected costing assumptions are given beside its measures, and Plan B's bases are in a table at the end of chapter 3. I can provide the detailed calculations.
