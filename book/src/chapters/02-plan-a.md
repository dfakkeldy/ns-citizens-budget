# Plan A: make the government's plan real

## Government's own costs first

These count toward the government's own $590 million target. They say where part of it can come from, starting at head office, without touching the front line.

| Measure | 2027-28 | 2029-30 |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Second civil-service step: attrition, head office first | $24M | $71M |
| Broader public sector 3%, management and administration first | $20M | $55M |
| Consultants capped; no contracting out of work left by attrition | $15M | $30M |
| Competitive tendering by default; sole-source awards published | $3M | $8M |
| Non-essential advertising over $50,000 paused | $1.5M | $2M |
| Premier's Office back to its 2025-26 budget | $1.5M | $2M |
| Information technology growth held; cyber security protected | $4M | $8M |
| Invest NS overhead held until the Auditor General reports | $3.5M | $3.7M |
| No repeat payroll rebates for large established firms, for now | $1.5M | $5M |
| Yarmouth-Maine ferry renewed under a capped contract | $2M | $4M |
| NSLC and NSGC cost restraint, with no change to prices or service | $3.5M | $9M |
| One more point of head-office attrition, once | $6M | $12M |
| **Named toward the $590 million** | **$85.5M** | **$209.7M** |

These are targets, not guarantees: each counts only once the position, contract or program is identified. $24 million is my even phasing of the government's own figure ("$95 million by year four" [8]), and the broader-sector row puts amounts on its announced 3% cuts; the rest is restraint I propose. No layoffs are needed to deliver them.

::: {.rail label="Attrition"}
The Budget Address announced civil-service cuts of 5% a year [8]. Deliver the second step through retirements, attrition and vacant positions only, starting with the roughly 133 positions that ten departments are funded for above their 2025-26 forecast staffing [10]. Head-office and management roles go first; front-line, rural, inspection and enforcement roles are exempt. Publish positions by department every quarter. Counting a vacancy as a saving only works where the plan would otherwise pay for it. The extra point of attrition is about 106 of this year's 10,559.5 funded positions [10], at about $112,000 each with benefits (my estimate): $6 million for half of 2027-28, then $12 million a year.
:::

::: {.rail label="Broader sector"}
The Budget Address announced 3% cuts for the broader public service [8], and CBC reports the four-year plan repeats them each year [63]. Deliver the next steps from management and administration, by attrition and vacancies, with no classroom, teaching or care position, and publish results by organization. This year NSCC put its grant cut at $9.4 million, although its Estimates line fell $1.9 million [63][10]; regional centres for education cancelled 47 vacant positions and laid off 13.5, about $5 million by my estimate [63]; and Legal Aid's 3% is $706,000 [11]. I assume the steps yield $20 million in 2027-28, $40 million in 2028-29 and $55 million in 2029-30.
:::

::: {.rail label="Consultants"}
Professional services in the consolidated Other Government segment reached $303.8 million in 2025-26, up about 33% in a year [4]. That total is not all consulting, so identify the avoidable management-consulting contracts before setting a cap; keep necessary legal, technical and service-delivery work. Post every consulting contract over $100,000.
:::

::: {.rail label="Tendering"}
$273 million of the Province's $1.8 billion in purchasing was non-competitive in 2024-25 [9]. Make competition the default, publish every sole-source award within 30 days, and re-tender expiring ones. Both opposition parties, the NSGEU and the Auditor General have criticized untendered contracts or called for their disclosure. I assume $40 million to $60 million of non-health, non-competitive buying is re-tendered in 2027-28 at an illustrative 5% to 10% lower price, less overlap with the consultant cap, with more re-tendered each year as contracts expire.
:::

::: {.rail label="The centre"}
The Executive Council Office budget rose from $15.5 million to $16.9 million, and its funded staff stayed at 91 while departments cut about 5% [10]. Return it to its 2025-26 budget and restrain ministers' expenses.
:::

::: {.rail label="Invest NS"}
Hold Invest NS operating costs at the 2025-26 forecast ($27.0 million, not $31.1 million) until the Auditor General's audit of Invest NS is tabled [11]. Honour every signed payroll rebate, including recent defence-sector agreements. Approve no repeat rebate for large firms already established here until the audit reports; after that, require an independent, published test of whether the jobs would come anyway, and weigh payments avoided against tax revenue that might be lost. Published deals show rebates worth 55% to 78% of the new provincial tax Invest NS projects from them [11]. Research on US state and local incentives finds they tip only 2% to 25% of location decisions [14]; that is a reason for a Nova Scotia test, not proof the jobs would come anyway. The audit's announced scope does not include payroll rebates, so Invest NS should also publish results for past rebates.
:::

::: {.rail label="Ferry"}
Renew the Yarmouth-Maine ferry; don't cancel it. The Province pays the difference between eligible costs and ticket income, so rising ridership already lowers the subsidy [49]. Use the final economic impact study (February 2026) to negotiate the 2027 contract, publish its cap and terms, and meet no cap through higher fares or fewer sailings. In 2024, 29% of non-resident passengers who stayed overnight visited Cape Breton Island [49]. The $2 million target assumes the 2027-28 plan provides for the ferry at this year's $21.6 million [10]; if it does not, a renewal is a new cost.
:::

::: {.rail label="NSLC and NSGC"}
Ask the Nova Scotia Liquor Corporation and Nova Scotia Gaming Corporation to find controllable operating savings, with no change to prices, store hours or service. Budget day put Crown corporations inside the broader public-sector reduction, so I count this toward the target unless Finance confirms it is additional [11]. It is separate from the broader public-sector row above, which does not count either corporation.
:::

## Keep health savings in health

Health and Seniors' care drove most of this year's overrun [1]. These measures slow cost growth without cutting care, and the savings stay in Health to cover those pressures. They are conditional targets for avoiding overruns, not verified cash savings: each needs the activity, replacement cost and service outcome identified first.

| Measure | 2027-28 | 2029-30 |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Employed float pools replace agency nurses, shift for shift | $8M | $15M |
| Health-authority administration held flat | $6M | $15M |
| Results or sunset for non-clinical transformation spending | $10M | $20M |
| Compete what can be competed | $3M | $12M |
| Home First: patients waiting for care move home sooner | $2M | $5M |
| Film fund held to its envelope (overrun avoided, counted at $0) | $0 | $0 |
| **Overruns prevented** | **$29M** | **$67M** |

::: {.rail label="Agency nurses"}
Travel-nurse spending was $59 million in 2022-23, $154 million in 2023-24 and $123 million in 2024-25, plus $12.7 million for other travel professionals [15]. Agency rates ran $56.50 to $100 an hour, plus per diems and travel for travel nurses, against $40.55 to $51.74 for staff registered nurses before benefits [16]. Grow Nova Scotia Health's 13-nurse internal travel team [17] into a provincial float pool that replaces agency shifts one for one, so no rural shift goes unfilled. Publish agency spending monthly, with a target of 20% to 30% below 2024-25.
:::

::: {.rail label="Administration"}
Hold the health-authority administration line flat at its 2026-27 level ($152.6 million) through vacancies and attrition in human resources, finance and communications, with no clinical layoffs. Nova Scotia Health's audited corporate support rose 29.6% in one year ($300.9 million to $389.9 million), although it stayed below its own budget [18]. Explain that increase before setting a deeper target.
:::

::: {.rail label="Transformation"}
Province-wide health transformation spending rose from $277.1 million in 2023-24 to $492.5 million in 2024-25 [15]. That total includes clinical work, so it is not a waste estimate. Every initiative over $1 million gets published targets; non-clinical items that miss them end. Surgical access, primary-care teams and the new health record system are protected, and half of any savings go to primary and home care. I assume $20 million of non-clinical savings in 2027-28, rising to $40 million by 2029-30; with half reinvested, $10 million and $20 million are counted.
:::

::: {.rail label="Competition"}
Act on the Auditor General's March 2026 recommendations, which Nova Scotia Health accepted, on the more than $3.7 billion it has awarded through alternative (non-competitive) procurement since 2020 [19]. The audit did not measure value for money, so it supports better controls rather than a measured saving rate. Honour the rights suppliers hold under signed contracts, and apply competition to future awards and to material additions beyond what signed contracts already provide. Nova Scotia Health has agreed to schedule competitive re-tendering when contracts reach their natural end [19]. Where it can choose whether to extend or renew a contract, I propose it first check whether re-tendering would give better value. Report the outcome of the virtual-care tender, with patient services continuing throughout.
:::

::: {.rail label="Home First"}
In 2023-24, 21.3% of Nova Scotia hospital days were designated alternate level of care, against 16.9% nationally: patients who no longer needed acute hospital care and were waiting for a more appropriate setting, such as long-term care, rehabilitation or home with support [20]. Home First works on the share who could go home with support. My illustrative targets cut alternate level of care days by 15,000 in 2027-28, rising to 35,000 in 2029-30, below the gap to the national rate (about 40,000 days a year). Only days where a surge bed closes count, at an assumed $300 a day saved after paying for replacement care. Half of that saving goes to home care; the other half is the target: $2 million, rising to $5 million. Fund discharge coordination and home support, and retire temporary surge beds only after the patients waiting in them have moved home or into long-term care. If freed beds are refilled, the gain is access, not cash.
:::

::: {.rail label="Film fund"}
Keep the $39.4 million fund whole with no mid-year top-ups; it was forecast at $69.0 million for 2025-26 against a $39.3 million estimate [10]. Enforce the published $10 million per-project maximum unless an exception is documented, keep the Distant Location Incentive for rural and Cape Breton shoots, and publish the program review the guidelines required by 2025-26.
:::

## Beyond the plan: what can honestly be counted

Very little can be honestly scored below the plan without cutting services or raising revenue. I count only two items:

- **Pacing capital (+$1.6 million in 2027-28, +$7.9 million by 2029-30).** Cap the capital contingency at its 2025-26 level ($45.0 million, against $69.4 million this year [10]) and defer about $50 million a year of non-urgent information technology and head-office building projects beyond 2029-30, starting in Halifax. Together I assume about $75 million a year less borrowing, against a 2027-28 capital plan that is not yet published; if that plan already assumes the slippage, the saving is zero. The $225 million deferred by 2029-30 is delayed, not cancelled. No hospital, long-term care home, school, public housing, water and sewer for housing, highway safety or rural service building is delayed. The saving is interest only, at the illustrative 4.2% financing rate.
- **The cost of delivering the housing and growth measures (−$3 million in 2027-28, −$2 million by 2029-30).** A provisional allowance for administration (evaluation, advisers, public-land site work and suite-loan administration), shown openly rather than "absorbed". Loans, land value and any purchases are outside these figures and need their own costed allocation before any pilot launches; Finance should set each pilot's cap and funding source.

These are asks that I score at $0 until Finance shows a real saving:

- **Itemize the Restructuring Costs line** ($527.7 million budgeted this year [10]) into wage settlements (in aggregate), workforce adjustment, legal settlements including Bill 148, and a capped amount for other initiatives. This year's forecast found $24.0 million of the line unneeded but cut expected restructuring recoveries by $70.1 million, so the line is a net $46.1 million worse than budget [1].
- **Collect what is owed** across government, excluding income-assistance and disability overpayments, low-income fines and student loans. Collecting money already counted as revenue does not lower the deficit; report cash recovered separately from any genuine new income.
- **Publish Treasury's annual assessment of the Public Debt Management Fund.** The fund held about $1.05 billion at March 2026 and the plan assumes no withdrawals [3][4]. Using part of it to retire maturing debt could save about $1 million a year if the spread is positive, but the Province keeps it as a liquidity buffer in a heavy borrowing year, so I count nothing.
- **Pilot two small checks.** Retune controls and schedules in government buildings that already have energy audits, and add pre-payment checks on physician claims under the existing agreement. Each might be worth a few hundred thousand dollars a year; count them only once measured.

## Rules that make it stick

1. **A published ledger for the $590 million.** Budget 2027-28 shows it by department and measure, continuing and new, with front-line and administrative positions, a service-impact note and a distributional analysis, updated every quarter. It says up front what will not be cut (chapter 5).
2. **A vote on over-budget spending.** Amend sections 27 and 28 of the Finance Act so the House approves additional appropriations above a set threshold, keeping an emergency process [51]. The Auditor General asked in 2022 for an assessment and has recommended the amendment since 2023; Finance has not accepted it [21]. Departments should show a 25% offset from their own non-front-line lines first. Physician services, pharmacare, income assistance, the Disability Support Program, home care, long-term care, emergency health services and disasters are exempt.
3. **Write the 40% guardrail into law**, with a published glide path back under it, a correction plan if it is breached (not automatic cuts), an annual report by the Auditor General, and the 2027-28 to 2029-30 capital plan published by category.
4. **Settle the Bill 148 remedy openly.** Negotiate within the court's window and book it explicitly. CBC reports the Premier put it in the hundreds of millions, and he said "it's not in the budget" [22].
5. **Budget demand programs at their real level.** Home care was budgeted at $443.3 million for 2026-27, below its $497.7 million 2025-26 forecast [1][10]. Set estimates from the run-rate so overruns become visible choices.
6. **Pay grants as needed, not up front**, under the recommendation Finance accepted in 2024. Of $432.7 million of over-budget grant spending the Auditor General tested, paid in March 2022 and March 2023, as much as $371.6 million was still unspent by recipients in December 2023; one payment was $6.4 million up front to my own county for a coastal trail [21].
7. **Explain the one-offs**: the $80.0 million gain on disposal of Crown assets booked for 2026-27, the aggregate 2027-28 compensation assumption, and next year's Restructuring Costs provision.
8. **The Dalhousie onshore-gas program ends at its announced $30 million total [53].** Any future money is paid against published milestones, with audit rights and the return of unused funds.
9. **No new information technology lock-in.** Core government paid Microsoft $37.7 million in 2025-26, up from $8.5 million in 2017-18 [64]. The Province is choosing a Microsoft licensing partner (the reseller): the tender closed on 1 October 2026, and no award had been posted by 3 October [64]. That competition must follow its published terms. Seats, prices and term are set by the Microsoft agreement behind it; I have not found its renewal date or verified its seat-reduction and exit rights (question 11). For every new solicitation and renewal, including that agreement, state before bids close a term of three years or less, counting any extensions, the right to drop seats, a price cap and data-exit terms. Make no other proprietary software commitment longer than three years without a costed exit plan. Publish a yearly register of software licences, seats and contract end dates, and default to open formats and open-source platforms for new systems. A two-year, 1,500-seat pilot of open-source office, mail and file-sharing software, shared with another public body, would test whether a wider move pays. I estimate an illustrative net cost of $1 million to $6 million over the two years. It is not in my figures: start it once Finance has costed it and set its cap, within the CSDS budget and without drawing on any saving counted here. Scale it only if running costs come in under about $250 a seat a year.

Plan A assumes the government's targets are delivered in full ($590.0 million, $724.5 million and $882.0 million [2]), and essentially matches the government's path: $1.4 million above it in 2027-28 and $6 million below it by 2029-30. Its value is a published, deliverable plan, not a lower deficit. If this year's overruns recur (September's recurring pressures: Health $125.3 million, Seniors' care $94.6 million and debt servicing $15.5 million), Plan A trims 2027-28 from $1.384 billion to $1.356 billion; it does not remove the stress. That case is a stress test, not a forecast. Net debt stays above 40% of the economy in every year: about 45.7% in 2029-30, counting $225 million of capital delayed beyond 2029-30.
:::
